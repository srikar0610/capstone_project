# CloudServe support system

Author: praveenkumarkundarapu

This report follows the section order in the submission guide. Tables and the architecture figure are numbered and referred to in the text.

## 1. Executive summary

CloudServe asked for a chatbot. The development tickets say the support queue is mostly a search failure. Of 500 labelled tickets, 357 (71.4%) are already answered in 29 knowledge-base articles, while historical first-contact resolution is 43.8% and the reported reply time is 8 to 12 hours against a 2-hour agreement. The system built here replies by quoting a retrieved passage and citing it, and it escalates everything else with the passage, the predicted intent, the confidence and a reason. It does not call a model unless that option is turned on.

On 21 November 2026 the harness processed all 80 validation tickets in one unattended run: 62 sent, 18 escalated, none blocked, decisions logged equal to tickets processed. That is not the hidden set of 120. The hidden file is not in the pack and has been run zero times. The same run shows a 29 percentage-point gap in automatic-reply rate between customer tiers, which misses the fairness limit of 5 points. That gap is reported rather than tuned away.

Tools used to write and review code are declared in section 10. The judgement about what may be sent automatically comes from the labels and the five transcripts.

## 2. The problem

Marcus Adeyemi asked for something that would answer the easy tickets so that six agents could spend their time on the hard ones. The service agreement is a first reply inside two hours. He reports eight to twelve. He said first-contact resolution, at 42%, is the number he is actually judged on, and that a confidently wrong answer to an engineering customer would be a failure even if the clock improved.

Sofia Restrepo, who works the queue, described a different bottleneck. About seven in ten tickets are questions she has already answered that month. The minutes go on finding the article and writing it out. The internal search matches titles, and customers do not use Ines Varga's titles. Daniel Okonkwo receives escalations that are often the original ticket with no note, and he said about half of them were tier-1 work. Ines has 29 reviewed articles and can see agents reconstructing them from memory. Ravi Menon, a customer, already searches those articles himself and then waits until the next day for the same text, unless the ticket is a failed deployment, in which case the wait is a stopped release.

Table 1, in the next section, puts those accounts next to the file. The client asked for a conversational system. The evidence asks for a documentation router with a written reason whenever a person must still be involved. A chatbot does not make that distinction. It will answer a security incident if the wording is polite, and it will not name the article.

The request and the job come apart in the interviews if they are read in order rather than mined for a solution. Marcus described a capacity problem. Six agents, more than five hundred tickets a week, two hires last year whose effect lasted about a month, a canned-response library the agents hated because finding the right canned text took longer than writing the answer, and outsourcing quotes that cost more than the team. He was explicit that he does not have a strong view of the mechanism. He wants the response-time number to come down without three more people he will not be given. He was also explicit about the number he actually cares about. Response time is what the agreement forces him to report. First-contact resolution is what the escalations cost. He said a ticket that bounces to tier two costs roughly four times a resolved one and makes the customer angrier than the wait itself. The industry figure people quote at him is sixty-five percent. The pack's target is sixty. His veto is a wrong answer that an engineer will screenshot. His second failure mode is a system that creates work, which is what the canned responses did. He could not break the queue down by topic, and he said so. He added two constraints that a chatbot design usually ignores: enterprise customers will notice if their service gets worse, and an autumn compliance review means every decision has to be explainable after the fact.

Sofia described the same queue from inside a morning. Forty to seventy tickets, sorted by age because the oldest are about to breach the agreement. A familiar ticket takes four or five minutes. An unusual one takes about forty and is escalated anyway. She estimated that seven in ten are questions she has already answered that month: password lockouts, rate limits, invoices, deployments that keep rolling back. The time is not spent deciding the answer. It is spent finding it and writing it out. She said the documentation is good and that search is painful, so agents keep personal files. She mentioned the search problem to Ines once and believes Ines heard it as a complaint that the articles were bad. Her escalation rule is three parts: she does not know, she is not confident enough to risk a wrong send, or it is security, which is always escalated. She is not worried about being replaced. She is worried that an automatic wrong answer becomes her apology. A draft with the relevant page attached would, in her words, save half of every ticket. She also said non-fluent tickets take longer because she has to work out the question before she can answer it, and that those tickets have the worst satisfaction. That last claim is testable, and section 3 shows that this file does not support it.

Daniel sees the escalations Marcus only sees as a rate. He said about half of what reaches him could have been closed in tier one with more confidence or the right page, and that Marcus does not see the inside of those tickets. What arrives is usually the original ticket, forwarded, with no summary and no note of what was already tried. He then reads the thread and often asks the customer something they were already asked. He said the frustration comes from that repetition more than from the wait. The help he asked for is specific: the ticket, a guessed topic, the documentation that looked relevant, and the reason the sender was not confident. He does not need the guess to be right. He needs the working shown. His warning about automation is that the system will learn what the team does now, including snippet files that were correct two years ago. He named the ticket types he does not want automated: security and account compromise, billing disputes that become contractual, and data location, where a human mistake is already a compliance problem.

Ines owns the twenty-nine articles. They are separate from the product documentation and cover authentication, deployment, the API, billing, data, security and account management. She reviews them on a rotation. External use is high. Internal use, she concluded after a year, is close to none: she sees her own paragraphs reconstructed from memory. The search matches titles and exact terms. A customer writes that a deployment keeps dying. Her article is titled around container health-check failures. Keyword search has no path between those phrases. She is more comfortable with retrieval over the reviewed articles than with the private files, on one condition: every answer names the article, so a wrong answer can be traced either to the article or to a misreading. Those are different fixes. She was clear about what has no article: feature requests, roadmap and timing, and novel incidents. The last group is small and is where a wrong answer does the most damage.

Ravi is a platform lead at a client of about two hundred people, on the business plan. He called the people good and the waiting the problem. A ticket raised at four in the afternoon is, in his experience, a next-day reply. The wait is not equal. A pagination question is annoying. A deployment failing at nine in the morning stops a release. While he waits he searches the documentation and finds the answer about half the time, then receives the same text hours later. He would accept an automated first response if it says what it thinks, names the page, and does not sound more certain than it is. He wants to know the reply was automatic, because he trusts a person's prose without checking and he will verify a machine draft. Hiding that distinction is what would annoy him. He also said a colleague on the enterprise plan gets answers in about an hour, and that a change which widens that gap will come up at renewal.

Read together, the five accounts are one problem seen from five seats. Marcus has the cost and the veto. Sofia has the repeated questions and the broken search. Daniel has the empty escalation. Ines has the articles and the citation requirement. Ravi has the unequal cost of waiting and the demand that an automatic reply identify itself. None of them said that 71 percent of the sample is already answered in those articles. That figure is what joins the five accounts, and it is why a chatbot is the wrong build. A chatbot optimises for a conversation. CloudServe is failing at delivery of a page it already wrote, and at explaining the cases it must not deliver automatically.

## 4. Requirements

Each requirement in Stage 2 cites a row of that evidence. The chain the marking scheme asks for is evidence, then requirement id, then the module.

| FR-CLS-1 | Intent, urgency, probability, alternatives | `src/classify.py` |

Out of scope, on purpose: a chat widget, automatic answers for the four never-auto intents, training on private snippet files, and a satisfaction score with no human reviewer. Version 1 of these requirements is not what shipped. Section 9 records the change.

The requirements are written so that two engineers would build broadly the same behaviour without being told the library names. FR-ING-1 says the four channels become one record and that a missing field is not an exception. It does not say which parser to use. The acceptance test posts one ticket from each channel, including the aliases "live chat", "community forum" and "documentation comment", and posts a non-object and an almost empty object. FR-CLS-1 says every ticket receives an intent, an urgency, a probability between zero and one, and the alternatives considered. Text that is too short to classify returns `unclear_request` rather than an exception, because an unclear ticket is already on the never-automatic list. FR-RET-1 says the identifiers must resolve to the twenty-nine articles and that a weak match returns nothing. Returning a vaguely related paragraph for every query would make the citation check look healthier than the retrieval is. FR-GEN-1 says the draft uses only those passages, cites them, and does not treat the customer text as an instruction. FR-RTE-1 says the decision is a function of the class, the confidence, the passages, the guardrail result and the kill switch, that the same inputs produce the same decision, and that the reason is a sentence a support manager can read. FR-GRD-1 says a guardrail blocks. A warning that still sends is not a guardrail, which is the build specification's wording and the reason the injection test checks both the flag and the customer-visible message. FR-LOG-1 lists the columns from the setup guide, including prompt version and requirement identifiers, because Marcus's compliance question is "why did it do that" months later. FR-RUN-1 says the command takes a path. A harness that opens `validation_tickets.json` by a hardcoded name cannot be pointed at the hidden file, and the instructions treat that as a failure of the gate.

The non-functional rows are how a demonstration becomes something that could be run by someone else. Latency is the ninety-fifth percentile, not the mean, because a chat customer experiences the slow case. Availability is the behaviour when the provider is missing, slow, or rate-limiting, and when the ticket is malformed. Accuracy is precision on a set the classifier was not fitted to, plus citation accuracy where a citation exists. Privacy is a count of outbound detections, with a target of zero. Auditability is an equality: logged final decisions equal tickets processed. Fairness is a gap under five percentage points across tier, region and fluency. Cost is the requirement that the unattended run not depend on a paid call. Section 7 shows which of these held on the visible validation file and which did not.

Assumptions were written down so that a later change would have a trigger. Version 1 assumed one model call could classify and answer, that 0.80 was the right send bar because a diagram in the brief uses it, that every high-urgency ticket should go to a person, and that the pinned LangChain and Chroma releases would install. Section 9 says which of those failed. The open questions that remain are whether the hidden file still looks like a 71 percent documentation problem, whether 0.40 is calibrated on tickets the classifier has not seen, and who at CloudServe is allowed to throw the kill switch. The third cannot be answered from the pack. The first two are answered by one stated run on the hidden file, not by another pass over the 80 tickets.

## 5. Architecture and design

Figure 1 is the path for every ticket.

```text
ticket -> ingest -> classify -> retrieve -> draft -> guardrails -> route -> log
```

Ingest maps the four channel names, including "live chat" and "community forum", onto one representation and keeps the original text. Classify is a logistic regression fitted only on `development_tickets.json`. Confidence is the predicted class probability. Retrieve is TF-IDF over 800-character windows of the 29 articles. Passages under a relevance threshold of 0.08 are dropped, because returning something for every query hides a miss. Generate quotes the remaining passages and appends the document id. Guardrails run on every outbound draft. Route sends only when the intent is allowed, confidence is at least 0.40, a passage cleared the threshold, no guardrail blocked, and the kill switch is off. The same inputs produce the same decision because none of those steps samples randomly.

The alternative that was rejected is the one in version 1 of the requirements: a single model call with a 0.80 threshold, and a Chroma index as in the setup guide. The threshold would not have moved the queue. The pinned Chroma release does not install on the interpreter used for this build. A local index also keeps the unattended run inside the free tier, which is a constraint of the project rather than a preference. The cost of that choice is that retrieval will miss paraphrases a neural embedding might have caught. The citation, when one is produced, is still a slice of a real article.

The API in `src/api.py` calls the same function as the harness, so a demonstration ticket and the file run do not diverge.

Figure 1 is deliberately a straight line. A graph of agents that can call each other would have been a closer match to the picture of a chatbot, and it would have made the acceptance tests harder to interpret. Each box has one job. Ingest does not classify. The classifier does not decide to send. The retriever does not invent a passage when the score is low. The draft does not get a second chance to talk its way past a guardrail. Route does not call a model to "see how it feels". The decision log is not a sample of interesting tickets. That separation is what the brief means by three layers under the six components: the policy can be read without reading the model client, and the model client can be left unused without changing the policy.

The retrieval design is a recorded choice, not a default that was never examined. The setup guide starts from 800-character chunks with 120 characters of overlap and says to try at least two configurations. The index here uses 800-character windows because a shorter window on these articles splits a procedure away from the sentence that says when to use it, and a much longer window mixes two procedures into one score. Overlap is implicit in the way neighbouring windows are cut from the same article rather than in a separate embedding model. The relevance floor is 0.08. Lower than that, a query with no real article still returns a passage and the citation looks successful. Higher than that, documented tickets start coming back empty and the escalation rate rises toward today's baseline, which is safe and which also fails the reason the project exists. The floor is an environment variable so the value that was used is the value in the log, not a number buried in a notebook. Identifiers are `doc_id` and `passage_id`. The passage text stored with the hit is a slice of the article, which is what makes A6 a check rather than a trust exercise.

Classification is a logistic regression on word features, trained on the 500 development tickets and no others. Confidence is the predicted probability of the chosen intent, not a separate score the model is asked to invent. The alternatives are the other class probabilities, so the log can show what was considered. Text shorter than twelve characters returns `unclear_request` at a confidence of 0.15. That fallback exists because the build specification says a classifier that cannot decide must not raise. It also exists because an exception on ticket forty of an unattended run is how A9 is failed. The closed list of twenty-two intents is a limitation, discussed in section 6. It is also why the validation precision of 1.0 should not be read as a general result: the validation tickets use the same intent inventory and similar wording. A ticket type that is not in the list cannot be discovered by this model. It can only be forced into a neighbour or into the fallback.

The threshold of 0.40 is the working cutoff after 0.80 was rejected. It is not a claim that 0.40 is well calibrated. Section 7 shows the calibration bins on the validation file, and those bins are too clean to be comforting. The other send conditions are independent of the number. The intent must not be security, compliance, a feature request or unclear. At least one passage must have cleared the relevance floor. Every guardrail must have returned not blocked. The kill switch must be off. High-urgency database issues and high-urgency performance degradation escalate even when the other conditions would allow a send. Removing any one of those conditions would change a different stakeholder promise. Removing the never-auto list would violate the labels. Removing the passage requirement would let a confident classification send an answer with nothing behind it. Removing the guardrail conjunction would let a blocked draft through if the class looked safe.

Two alternatives were rejected after they had been the plan. The first was a single completion that both labelled the ticket and wrote the customer email. It is the obvious reading of "build a chatbot", and it cannot satisfy a test that follows the citation into the article, because the model can name a document it did not use. The second was the setup guide's Chroma index with a downloaded embedding model. Those pins do not install on the interpreter used for this project, and the first unattended run would have depended on a large download. A local TF-IDF index is weaker on paraphrase. Ines's own example, "my deployment keeps dying" against an article about container health checks, is the kind of miss a neural embedding might have saved. The trade is recorded so that a later change to embeddings is a change of retrieval quality, not a silent rewrite of the citations. Until that change, a miss returns nothing and the ticket escalates, which is the behaviour the build specification prefers to a plausible wrong page.

Monitoring and continuous integration are part of the same design, not a week-three decoration. The API exposes `tickets_processed_total`, `guardrail_blocks_total` and `response_seconds` on port 8000 at `/metrics`. `prometheus.yml` scrapes that port. A starter Grafana dashboard is in `grafana/dashboard.json`. The GitHub Actions workflow installs `requirements.txt` and runs the test command from the README. Tests that need a live key are not in that workflow. The default configuration does not have a live key. That is how the pipeline stays runnable on a machine that is not this one.

## 6. Implementation

The difficult part was not the HTTP layer. It was keeping a citation honest. A fluent rewrite can mention a document id it did not use. The shipped draft therefore quotes the passage. The optional OpenRouter call, `USE_LLM=true`, is specified in `prompts/build/generate_v1.txt` and is off in `.env.example`. If it is turned on and the provider times out, returns 429, or has a placeholder key, the extractive draft is kept and the ticket still finishes. That is the behaviour acceptance criterion A11 asks for.

Guardrails block three things: instructions embedded in the ticket, private data patterns in the outbound text, and claims the passages do not support, including a refund already issued, a fix already applied, and a delivery date. A block escalates. The customer receives the handoff sentence, not the blocked draft.

What should be restructured later is the classifier's closed list of 22 intents. A hidden ticket with a new intent can only fall into that list or into `unclear_request`. The fallback exists. It is not the same as recognising a new category.

Tests are `python -m pytest tests/ -v`. On the last run in this workspace, 30 tests passed, including one test named for each of criteria A2 through A11. A1 is the README followed on a clean machine. A12 is that pytest command.

The implementation is organised as the setup guide asks, with a few extra modules that stay inside those folders. `src/pipeline.py` is the only place that knows the order of the stages. `src/config.py` reads the environment at call time, so a test can change `USE_LLM` or the kill switch without restarting the interpreter. `src/llm.py` is the optional client. It retries a 429 with backoff and raises a provider error on timeout, on a non-200 that is not a retry, and on a placeholder key. The pipeline catches that error and keeps the quoted draft. A ticket that still cannot be processed inside the harness becomes an escalation with the exception recorded, and the loop continues. That is the difference between a notebook that works when the cells are run in order and a run that can be left alone.

What was difficult was not FastAPI. It was stopping the reply from looking more certain than the passage. Quotation is a blunt way to do that. It will sometimes send a paragraph that is longer than a person would have written, and it will sometimes include a neighbouring sentence the customer did not need. It will not say that a refund has been issued unless those words are in the passage, and the guardrail blocks that sentence even if a later rewrite adds it. The patterns for private data are emails, card-like numbers and key-like tokens. They will miss a customer name written out in a sentence. The draft's first defence is that it does not copy the ticket into the reply at all. The pattern match is the second defence, and it blocks rather than redacting, because a redacted send is still a send of a draft that contained someone else's data.

What should be restructured if this were going to stay in production is the intent inventory and the fairness of the send decision. A new incident type in the hidden file has nowhere to go except a neighbouring class or `unclear_request`. The fallback is safe, because unclear tickets escalate, but only if the model admits uncertainty. A confident wrong neighbour would send a documented answer to the wrong problem. The tier and region gaps in section 7 are the other restructuring. They are not fixed by a paragraph in the report. They are fixed by reading the errors on the development file, without touching the validation file, and by deciding whether the threshold or the retrieval is what treats standard-tier and Latin American tickets differently. That work is named as next work in section 10 because doing it against the 80-ticket file would spend the only clean view of those tickets.

The daily checkpoints in the build specification were the order of construction. Ingest first, then retrieval that can be traced to an article, then classification and a log row with a reason, then a guardrail that actually blocks, then one command over the whole file. The harness was not left until the end. A system that handles many intents and has never processed more than a handful of tickets in sequence is the failure the specification describes. The 80-ticket run finishing with a metrics file is the evidence that this build did not stop at the handful. The hidden 120 is still ahead of it.

The figures above should be treated with caution because first-contact resolution here counts an automatic cited reply as resolved, customer satisfaction has not been scored by a person, and the fairness gaps say the system does not yet treat tiers and regions evenly. Meeting the resolution target while missing the fairness condition is not a deployable result. The governance rule in the framework is that the fairness condition overrides the business number.

Hallucination rate is not claimed. The automatic citation check does not read whether the sentence overclaims the paragraph. The evaluation framework asks for two people to review at least fifty replies. That review has not been done.

Table 3 should be read from the top, in the order the client would read it. Eighty tickets were processed, which is the volume row, and none were dropped. Sixty-two were answered from the documentation and eighteen were escalated. No guardrail fired on this particular file. That last fact does not mean the guardrails are absent. They are tested with tickets written to trigger them, which is how A7 is judged, and this validation sample did not contain those tickets. Treating a zero block count as evidence that the checks are unnecessary would be the mistake the specification lists: a guardrail that is switched off for the run. They were on. They had nothing in these eighty bodies that matched.

The business reading of 77.5 percent first-contact resolution is the number Marcus would ask for, and it needs the definition kept next to it. In the historical file, first-contact resolution means a person closed the ticket without a further handoff. In this run it means the system sent a cited reply and did not escalate. Those are not the same event. A sent reply that did not solve the problem becomes a repeat contact, and repeat contact is 21.6 percent in the history. This run cannot see the repeat, because there is no second week of customer behaviour. The 77.5 percent is therefore an upper bound on resolution, not a measured resolution. It is still the right direction against a 42 percent baseline, and it is above the 60 percent target only under that definition. The escalation rate of 22.5 percent is under the 30 percent target on the same definition. If a human review later decides that some of the sixty-two sends were wrong, both numbers move, and they move in the direction that matters.

Reply time in Table 3 is processing time. The median is a fraction of a second. The pack's target is under five minutes, against eight to twelve hours. Processing time is the right measure for an automatic reply that is sent when the decision is logged, and it is the wrong measure if a queue, a human approval step or a provider retry is later inserted. The report states the definition so that a five-minute operational delay cannot be hidden behind a millisecond histogram. Latency at the ninety-fifth percentile is 0.017 seconds on this machine for this file. That meets the three-second target and says nothing about a cold start that has to download an embedding model, which is one reason the embedding model was not part of the path.

Classification macro precision of 1.0 is reported because the code calculated it, not because it should be believed as a generalisation. Every labelled ticket in the eighty was assigned the intent in its label. The support per class is small, often a handful, which is why a macro average can sit at 1.0 without each class having been stressed. The development file is what the model was fitted on, and a metrics run pointed at that file would be a training score. The harness writes a warning when the input stem is `development_tickets`. The validation file is a different file. It is still drawn from the same population and the same twenty-two names. The hidden file is the first time the number is about tickets the project has not been able to stare at. Until that run, the honest technical claim is narrower: on these eighty, the classifier reproduced the labels, retrieval returned a passage for 92.5 percent of tickets, and where the label named an expected document the retrieved set hit it 92.45 percent of the time. Citation accuracy among the citations that were produced was 100 percent, meaning each citation's document id and passage id were in the retrieved set. That is the check A6 describes. It is not a reading of whether the sentence was the right length or the right emphasis.

Calibration is the check the evaluation framework says students skip, and the bins are in the metrics file. On this run every bin from 0.3 to 1.0 had an observed intent accuracy of 1.0. The stated confidence was lower than that: about 0.36 in the single ticket between 0.3 and 0.4, about 0.46 for two tickets, and about 0.85 for the forty-one tickets between 0.8 and 0.9. The model is under-confident relative to its accuracy on this file, if accuracy means "matched the label". Under-confidence makes a 0.40 threshold conservative. It does not prove the threshold is safe, because the labels may be easy and because a bin with one or two tickets cannot support a calibration claim. The framework's picture of a failure is the opposite case: a bin that says 0.85 and is right 60 percent of the time. That failure is not what these eighty show. It is what the hidden file might show, and the report will not claim it has been ruled out.

The fairness rows are the part of Table 3 that fails a condition the framework says overrides the other numbers. Automatic-reply rate by tier is 93.3 percent for business, 87.5 percent for enterprise, and 64.3 percent for standard. The gap is 29.0 points. By region it is 81.5 percent in North America, 81.0 percent in Asia Pacific, 76.0 percent in Europe and 57.1 percent in Latin America, a gap of 24.3 points. By fluency it is 77.1 percent fluent and 78.9 percent non-fluent, a gap of 1.9 points, which is inside the five-point limit. The fluency result is the one Sofia's interview would have predicted to be the worst, and it is the one that holds. The tier and region results are the ones Ravi's renewal comment would have predicted to matter, and they do not hold. Standard-tier customers and customers in Latin America are escalated more often. Enterprise is not the group being under-served by the automation on this file. Standard is. That is a different unfairness from the human baseline, where enterprise had the weakest first-contact resolution. Both can be true. The human process already treats enterprise differently, in Ravi's account by answering them faster. The automatic process treats standard and Latin American tickets more cautiously, or matches their wording less well. Either explanation is a reason not to declare the system fair. Neither explanation was "fixed" by moving the threshold after seeing this table. Moving it would fit the validation file. The gap is a release blocker under NFR-06, and it is left as a blocker.

Governance counts on the same run are the third tier of the framework. Eighty decisions were logged and the count matched the tickets. Private-data detections were zero. Guardrail activations by type were empty, for the reason already given. The kill switch was off. Model degradation was zero because the model was not called. A run with `USE_LLM=true` and a dead provider would increment the degraded count and would still be expected to finish. That path is covered by a test, not by this eighty-ticket file.

Table 4 is the per-class view that the macro average hides. Every class on this file was recovered exactly. Several classes have a support of one. A perfect score on one compliance ticket is not a claim about compliance tickets in general. It is a claim about that one ticket. The classes with more support, billing queries at ten and rollback requests, data residency, API keys and unclear requests at six, are still a small sample. The table is included so the 1.0 is visible as a stack of small perfect scores rather than as a single impressive number.

Three tickets are enough to show the behaviour the video has to demonstrate, and they are the same behaviours the tests lock in. The caution is not a footnote. It is the reason the 77.5 percent must not be the sentence that opens a conversation with Marcus. The operational definition of the gate is the one the build specification will actually execute. The repository is cloned into an empty directory. The README is followed in order. A key is substituted into the example environment if a key is required. On this build a key is not required. One ticket from each channel is submitted. A ticket written to trip a guardrail is submitted. The full file is started and left alone. The metrics file and the decision log are reconciled. The test command is run. The tree is searched for credentials. This project has been through the test command and the eighty-ticket file on the machine where it was built. It has not been through a clone onto a different machine by a different person. That rehearsal is still worth doing before upload, because the specification says about half of the failures are a README step that existed only on the author's machine. The commands that person must be able to run without invention are `python -m src.api`, `python -m evaluation.harness --input <path> --output <directory>`, and `python -m pytest tests/ -v`.

## 8. Governance and risk

Table 5. Risks and the control that is actually in the design.

| ID | Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation | Owner |
|----|------|------------|--------|------------|-------|
| R-01 | A fluent wrong answer is sent | Medium | High | Quote a passage or send nothing. Block unsupported claims. | Support engineering |
| R-02 | Private data in the outbound text | Low | High | The draft does not copy the ticket. A pattern match blocks and escalates. | Support engineering |
| R-03 | The ticket is obeyed as an instruction | Medium | High | The injection check blocks. The ticket is not concatenated into the system instruction. | Support engineering |
| R-04 | Some groups are answered less often | Already observed | Medium | The metrics file reports the gap. A gap over 5 points is a release blocker, and Table 3 fails it. | Support engineering |
| R-05 | An article goes stale | Medium over a quarter | High | The citation is the article id, so the writer can see which article was wrong. | Technical writing |
| R-06 | The model provider is down | Medium if the option is on | Low on the default path | The default path does not call a provider. A failed call keeps the draft. | Support engineering |
| R-07 | Latency under load | Low at this volume | Medium | The local path is milliseconds on 80 tickets. It has not been load-tested at 500 a week concurrent. | Support engineering |
| R-08 | Cost grows with volume | Low | Medium | No paid call on the default path. | Support engineering |

The kill switch is `KILL_SWITCH=true` in `.env`, then a restart of `python -m src.api`. Health check shows the flag. Tickets already in a running harness finish under the old process. New tickets escalate. The person authorised to use it in production has not been named by CloudServe. The test is to set the flag and confirm a previously sendable ticket escalates.

Incident order, for someone who has not read the code: detect from the metrics or a customer report, set the kill switch, read the latest `final` rows in `storage/decisions.db`, do not send a second automatic reply, leave the switch on until the article or the rule is identified, write down the time and the ticket ids.

This system must never send a reply that is not grounded in a retrieved passage, never send after a guardrail block, and never auto-answer the four forbidden intents. The most likely remaining harm is a passage that is on-topic and still the wrong fix, sent quickly to a standard-tier customer in a region the retriever matches less well. It should not be put in front of a real customer until the tier and region gaps have been examined on the development file, without fitting the validation file, and until a person has read a sample of replies.

The decision record is the minimum schema from the governance framework, stored as one SQLite row with stage `final` rather than as four rows that could drift apart. The identifier, the time, the ticket id, the prediction, the confidence, the threshold that was applied, the action, the reason, the sources, the guardrail checks, the prompt version and the requirement identifiers are all on that row. A later argument about whether a send was intended can be answered by reading the row, not by re-running the model and hoping the provider returns the same text. The harness counts those rows against the tickets it just processed and sets `decisions_match_tickets`. A path that finished a ticket without a row would show up as a false value. On 21 November 2026 that value was true.

Each risk in Table 5 has a control that is a property of the code rather than a hope about the model. A wrong answer is limited by quotation and by the unsupported-claim list. Private data is limited by not copying the ticket and by a block that escalates. An injection is limited by a check that looks for instructions aimed at the system, and by the fact that the customer text is not placed in the system role even when the optional model call is on. Unequal service is not limited by a control that forces the rates together. It is limited by a measurement that refuses to call the system ready while the gap is wide. Stale documentation is limited by the citation, which gives Ines a specific article to correct, and it is not limited by any automatic expiry. A dead provider is limited by not needing the provider. Latency under a real Monday morning is not limited by anything this project has measured beyond eighty sequential tickets. Cost is limited by the default configuration. The owner column says support engineering because this is an individual build standing in for that function. It is not a named employee at CloudServe, and the report does not invent one.

The incident procedure is written for someone who has not read the repository. Detect the problem from a customer report or from the metrics, in particular a rise in guardrail blocks or a tier gap that was not there last week. Contain it by setting `KILL_SWITCH=true` and restarting the API process, then confirm `/health` shows the flag. Assess by opening `storage/decisions.db` and reading the latest final rows: ticket id, action, reason, sources, guardrails. Do not send a second automatic reply on those ticket ids. Notify the person who owns the support queue, which in the interviews is Marcus, with the ticket ids and whether a reply already went out. Remediate the article or the rule, not the customer's thread, and leave the switch on until that change is identified. Review by writing down the time the switch was turned on and what changed before it was turned off. Tickets already inside a running harness finish on the old process. New tickets escalate. The switch is tested by setting it and confirming that a ticket which previously sent now escalates. There is no separate deployment step, which is the requirement that the switch be usable at two in the morning.

The declaration the framework asks for at the end of the governance document is the same as the opening of this section. The system must never send an ungrounded reply, a blocked draft, or an automatic answer on the four forbidden intents. The mechanism is the guardrail module together with the never-auto list and the kill switch. The most likely remaining harm is a relevant-looking passage that is the wrong fix, delivered faster to some customer groups than to others. Deployment to a real customer waits on a fairness reading that does not fit the validation file, and on a human sample of the replies.

## 9. The requirements revision

Version 1 said the system would call a model once and would send only above 0.80, because that number appears in the brief. Building showed that a 22-way probability rarely reaches 0.80, so the queue would have stayed at eight to twelve hours, and that a model sentence could not satisfy a citation check that opens the article. Version 2 quotes passages, uses 0.40, records that threshold on the decision row, and hard-escalates security, compliance, feature requests and unclear tickets. High-urgency database and performance tickets also escalate, because those were the high-urgency intents a person still handled. High-urgency deployment failures were not.

The discovery count of 71.4% would have made the model-call design look wrong earlier. It was in the workbook as a statistic before it was treated as a constraint. That is the change of mind the project is for.

## 10. Conclusions

What to do next is a fairness pass on the development file by tier and region, a human review of fifty replies, and one unattended run on the hidden file with the date written down. What remains uncertain is whether 0.40 is calibrated on tickets the classifier has not seen, and whether the hidden file is still about 70% documentation. If it is not, the automatic rate should fall. That would be the system working.
